LLM-Memory
AI Models Can Predict and Collaboratively Modulate Human Memory Search
Eric Lacosse, Mariana Duarte, Graham Todd, Peter M. Todd, Daniel C. McNamee · arXiv:2608.26152 (v2, 28 Aug 2026), Indiana University LLM-Memory KV-Networks

Approach: Uses the semantic fluency task (SFT) — generate as many members of a category as possible — as a controlled assay of generative semantic memory retrieval. The authors test whether an LLM can track and predict the trajectory of an individual human's memory search, benchmarked against human-to-human prediction, and then ask whether the model can be used interactively to modulate (extend) that search.

Finding: The LLM's ability to track and predict human memory trajectories exceeds that of other humans. The result positions LLM-derived semantic structure as a candidate computational model of human memory search — and, more provocatively, as an interactive scaffold that can push a person's search into regions of semantic space they would not have reached unaided.

⚠ Preprint, not peer reviewed. The headline comparison ("exceeds other humans") warrants scrutiny: an LLM trained on aggregate corpus statistics may predict the typical trajectory well while failing on idiosyncratic, experience-specific paths — which is precisely where episodic rather than semantic structure should dominate.
What It Costs to Compose, Rebuild, and Correct Precomputed Memory
Asa Shepard · arXiv:2608.30647 (31 Aug 2026) LLM-Memory KV-Networks

Approach: Treats a saved KV cache over a corpus — "precomputed memory," a model's saved reading of material, reused across queries — and trained compressions of it as a memory store, then measures three operations that any deployed memory must support: composing memories prepared separately, rebuilding after the underlying material changes, and correcting by serving new information alongside the memory. Experiments use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

Finding: All three operations are fragile in specific ways. Composed precomputed memories degrade relative to memories computed over the joint material; staying current requires rebuilds costing a large fraction of full preparation; and corrections served beside a memory are simply ignored, conditional on phrasing. Warm-rebuilding trained compressions, and serving specifically-phrased updates as pasted text or injected cache state, are the most promising remedies. The practical implication is that precomputed memory should be rebuilt on the cadence at which new information changes what the memory was computed from.

⚠ Single-author preprint, single 8B model, no independent replication. The phrasing-dependence of correction uptake is the most interesting result and also the one most likely to be an artifact of prompt construction.
Schema-Episodic
Assimilation of newly learned category features in the ventromedial prefrontal–anterior hippocampus system
Jingzhao Yuan, Fang Yue, Peijuan Li, …, Carol A. Seger · NeuroImage, September 2026 (online June 2026) Schema-Episodic 📚 matches-library

Approach: fMRI during two-phase family-resemblance category learning. Participants first learned a category (during which most adopt an abstract prototype strategy), then learned new features for the already-learned category — Piaget's "assimilation" — followed by a transfer test. Controlled (trial-based) design, not naturalistic.

Finding: Before new-feature acquisition, prototype representations were present in vmPFC and anterior hippocampus. During new-feature learning, vmPFC and anterior hippocampus were more active than during initial learning, and at transfer, participants using a prototype strategy again showed prototype representations in vmPFC and anterior hippocampus — whereas exemplar representations recruited intraparietal sulcus and caudate instead. The double dissociation is the point: abstract prototypes are carried by a declarative, schema-like system (vmPFC–anterior hippocampus), not by the procedural/exemplar system, and that schema system flexibly assimilates new features rather than requiring the category to be relearned.

Between the lines: Role of cultural schemas in remembering and forgetting
Nan Peng, Robert H. Logie, Sergio Della Sala · Memory & Cognition, 28 Aug 2026 Schema-Episodic

Approach: Four experiments using coherent narrative prose with cued recall at immediate and delayed retention intervals. In Experiments 1–2, participants' interpretation of the cultural properties implied by the narrative indexed cultural-schema activation; in Experiments 3–4, activation was manipulated directly by presenting culturally familiar versus unfamiliar versions.

Finding: Activated cultural schemas facilitated recall of coherent narrative at both immediate and delayed tests. The shape of forgetting was negatively accelerating and better fit by a power function than alternatives, in both group and individual data. Critically, the rate of forgetting was independent of initial degree of learning — schema activation raises the intercept of the forgetting function without changing its slope. Schemas buy you a better starting representation, not a slower decay.

The contribution of semantic control to elaborative episodic encoding
Fiona Lancelotte, Alexa Morcom · Memory & Cognition, 1 Sep 2026 Schema-Episodic Encoding-Retrieval

Approach: Two preregistered experiments (N = 78; N = 64) in young adults. Participants studied object images under one of two semantic encoding tasks: controlled semantic retrieval (choose the word most related in meaning) versus semantic selection (choose the word most similar in size). Memory was tested for concept-level item recognition, old-lure discrimination, and source memory; a hierarchical Bayesian multinomial processing tree model decomposed source memory parameters.

Finding: Controlled semantic retrieval at encoding improved concept-level recognition relative to semantic selection but did not affect specific memory (old-lure discrimination). Greater control demand — indexed by lower word2vec image–word similarity — slowed encoding judgements but had no significant effect on later memory. The modeling showed better source-memory detection following controlled retrieval encoding. So elaborative encoding via controlled retrieval buys gist-level and source-level memory, but not the fine-grained detail that distinguishes a studied item from a related lure.

⚠ Young adults only; the control-demand manipulation produced a null effect on all memory measures, so the "demand" half of the design is uninformative as reported.
KV-Networks
Structural generalization and continual learning enabled by factorized entorhinal-hippocampal memory and entorhinal-parietal action circuits
Jaedong Hwang, Sujaya Neupane, Mehrdad Jazayeri, Ila Fiete · bioRxiv, 25 Aug 2026 KV-Networks Cross-cutting

Approach: Combines behaviour, neural recordings, and modeling in an abstract sequential image navigation task that dissociates three generalizations: mnemonic (visual → mental navigation), transitive (trained → novel routes), and structural (familiar → new environments). Neural activity was recorded from entorhinal cortex and posterior parietal cortex in two monkeys.

Finding: Generic recurrent neural networks failed at all three generalizations, whereas monkeys and humans succeed. A structured entorhinal-hippocampal memory model — a content-independent metric scaffold based on grid cells for storing experience — coupled to a separate policy RNN succeeded at all three. The scaffold supports mnemonic and transitive generalization through path integration, enables structural generalization by letting a previously learned action policy be reused, and its high combinatorial capacity permits continual learning without catastrophic forgetting. EC and PPC populations carried two distinct computations; modularizing them into separate distance-tracking and action-initiation networks captured the population dynamics and improved performance. An RL module learned a scale factor aligning grid periodicity to the environment's temporal structure.

⚠ Preprint, not peer reviewed. The monkey electrophysiology rests on n = 2 animals.
Subicular spatial codes arise from predictive mapping
Lauren Bennett, William de Cothi, Laurenz Muessig, …, Steven Poulter, Caswell Barry · Nature Communications, 31 Aug 2026 KV-Networks Cross-cutting 📚 matches-library

Approach: Computational modeling study. The successor representation (SR) has explained place cells and grid cells, but subicular neurons — the main hippocampal output — show heterogeneous spatial responses that have resisted a unified account. The authors incorporate rodent behavioural biases into the SR and compare it against purely spatial and boundary-vector-cell models of subiculum.

Finding: The behaviourally-informed SR reproduces the heterogeneous activity patterns of subicular neurons and — notably — accounts for the emergence of boundary and corner cells, cell types absent from upstream hippocampal regions. Subicular firing is better described by SR than by spatial or BVC models. The work also establishes a temporal hierarchy in hippocampal–subicular processing: subiculum encodes predictive representations over longer time horizons than CA1, capturing extended behavioural patterns and environmental affordances. This is a clean instance of a single normative predictive code generating cell types that look like a zoo of unrelated tuning curves.

Encoding-Retrieval
Active control modulates memory-related activity during encoding and encoding–retrieval similarity
Zhuolei Ding, Shuge Yuan, Jincheng Xu, Shudong Zhang, Simon Hanslmayr, Xun Liu, Mingxia Zhang · bioRxiv, 23 Aug 2026 (Beijing Normal University / University of Glasgow) Encoding-Retrieval Schema-Episodic

Approach: EEG recorded while participants encoded objects under an active (self-directed) versus yoked (matched passive) condition, then again during a delayed recognition test roughly 24 hours later. ERPs were examined across early windows (pre-stimulus slow potential, N2) and late windows (P300, late slow wave, post-stimulus slow potential), plus encoding–retrieval similarity (ERS) between neural patterns at encoding and retrieval.

Finding: Active control improved delayed recognition, with the advantage expressed selectively for detailed recognition. Early processing windows showed classic subsequent-memory effects — pre-stimulus slow potential and N2 separated later-remembered from later-forgotten items — but these were not modulated by active control. Active control modulated only the late effects: remembered–forgotten differences in late stimulus-related and immediate post-stimulus processing appeared only in the active condition. ERS showed the same signature, with memory-related encoding–retrieval similarity present under active but absent under yoked learning, in relatively late windows. Supports a constructive-processing account: active control builds richer, more detailed encoding representations that can actually be reinstated at retrieval.

⚠ Preprint, not peer reviewed. The yoked design is the strength here; the null ERS under yoked learning is the load-bearing result and would benefit from an equivalence test rather than a null comparison.
Prioritization in working memory reduces interference via beta band-linked stimulus transformations
Jacqueline M. Fulvio, Bradley R. Postle · Journal of Neuroscience, 1 Sep 2026 Encoding-Retrieval

Approach: A two-step retrocuing task in which participants hold two items in working memory and the item not cued by the first cue cannot be dropped, because the second cue may prioritize it. Experiment 1 used dense sampling of recall performance over time; Experiment 2 sought EEG correlates.

Finding: Recall performance oscillated at ~15 Hz ("low-beta") in the prioritization task versus ~20 Hz ("higher-beta") in a matched neutral-cue task. The prioritized item was shielded from bias exerted by the uncued item and by items carried over from the previous trial. The prioritization cue uniquely triggered a phase reset at 15 Hz and an increase in low-beta oscillatory peaks; burst analysis ruled out bursting as the mechanism, and self-correlation showed the cue triggered greater whole-topography reorganization than a neutral cue. The interpretation is that shielding arises from transforming the non-prioritized item into an active "unprioritized" state maintained by a low-beta mechanism — not from simply dropping it.

Cross-cutting
Orthogonal representational geometry in dACC underpins human hierarchical reasoning
Chuanyong Xu, Ning Mei, Wenshan Dong, Rui Hu, Tom Verguts, Qi Chen · Cell Reports 45(9):117929, 1 Sep 2026 Cross-cutting 📚 matches-library

Approach: Combines Bayesian modeling and recurrent neural network simulation of hierarchical reasoning with human neuroimaging. Tasks are hierarchically structured, with high-level rules governing low-level perceptual judgments, so good performance requires reasoning about the rule level rather than the stimulus level.

Finding: Accumulated errors and perceptual difficulty are integrated in dorsal anterior cingulate cortex to estimate high-level rule-switch confidence. These two variables are represented along two separable dimensions approximable by basis functions, and orthogonality between them supports a two-dimensional representation well suited to hierarchical reasoning. Crucially, a closer-to-orthogonal representational geometry correlates with more accurate computation and better task performance — geometry is doing computational work here, not merely decorrelating nuisance variables.

Reduced astroglial-synapse coverage on cortical non-engram cells shapes remote memory strength
Aline Mak, Cristina Boers-Escuder, Sandra Parisi, Twinke M. Blok, August B. Smit, Michel C. van den Oever, Mark H.G. Verheijen · Cell Reports 45(9):117828, 1 Sep 2026 Cross-cutting

Approach: Introduces Astro-GRASP, a genetically targeted method for probing astrocyte–synapse interactions, applied in mouse prelimbic cortex. The authors tracked perisynaptic astrocytic process (PAP) coverage separately for engram versus non-engram neurons at recent versus remote timepoints after fear conditioning, then depleted ezrin from astrocytes to test causality.

Finding: Fear conditioning globally increased astrocyte–synapse coverage during recent consolidation, but at the remote timepoint astrocytes selectively retracted from non-engram synapses. Ezrin depletion reduced local coverage and enhanced remote memory expression. The counterintuitive upshot: astroglial coverage of non-engram — not engram — synapses shapes remote memory strength, implying that withdrawal of glial support from competing synapses sharpens the expressed engram. This adds a non-neuronal, competitive mechanism to systems consolidation.

Manifold-constrained plasticity enables stable learning in recurrent neural circuits
Camille Godin, Jean-Philippe Thivierge · PLOS Computational Biology, 25 Aug 2026 Cross-cutting

Approach: Addresses the problem that population activity is confined to low-dimensional manifolds that drift over time, while standard learning rules implicitly assume stable, full-rank representations. The proposed rule, SPLiT (Synaptic Projection Learning with intrinsic Tracking), uses an online Oja rule to continuously estimate the intrinsic low-dimensional activity subspace and performs normalized least-mean-squares learning in manifold coordinates. Tested in rate-based recurrent networks under both constrained dynamics (activity restricted to a fixed manifold) and unconstrained dynamics (drifting dominant subspace).

Finding: SPLiT reliably learns time-varying target signals in both regimes, with faster convergence, reduced sensitivity to recurrent gain, smaller weight updates, and greater robustness to noisy teaching signals than baselines. Analytically, SPLiT learns the optimal decoder projected onto the instantaneous principal subspace and maintains bounded error under drift — a mechanistic account of how plasticity can exploit manifold structure to keep learning stable despite representational drift.

⚠ Purely simulation work — no comparison against biological plasticity or neural recording data, so the claim is about a possible mechanism rather than an identified one.
Noradrenergic infraslow rhythm during sleep is the critical link between heart-rate dynamics and memory consolidation
Sofie S. Jacobsen, Allison B. Morehouse, Pin-Chun Chen, …, Celia Kjaerby · eLife, 28 Aug 2026 Cross-cutting

Approach: Cross-species design. Optogenetics in mice establishes causality for the locus coeruleus (LC) → heart rate (HR) relationship during NREM; parallel human sleep recordings test whether infraslow HR variability indexes spindle-dependent memory processing, using overnight retention as the behavioural readout.

Finding: HR fluctuations during NREM are tightly phase-locked to noradrenergic infraslow (~0.02 Hz) rhythms, identifying LC as a key driver of very-low-frequency HR variability. Transient LC inhibition blunts HR slowing and LC activation produces rapid acceleration; the relationship holds across a defined range of LC activity but breaks down when LC activity becomes excessive — a graded, non-monotonic link. In mice, the amplitude of NREM HR decelerations correlates with spindle activity and subsequent memory performance, and human sleepers show the same pattern: stronger VLF-HR fluctuations track increased spindle expression and better overnight retention. Infraslow HR variability thus becomes a non-invasive peripheral marker for brainstem-driven consolidation.

Peripheral
Single-Unit Activity in the Human Medial Temporal Lobe Reflects Reduced Error Monitoring During Mind Wandering
Thomas P. Reber, Leila Chaieb, Marlene Derner, …, Juergen Fell · Hippocampus, 27 Aug 2026 Peripheral 📚 matches-library

Approach: Single-unit recordings from the medial temporal lobe of presurgical epilepsy patients performing an attention task with interspersed experience-sampling probes, allowing periods of mind wandering to be identified from the participant rather than inferred.

Finding: Behaviourally, error adjustment was reduced during mind-wandering periods relative to task-focused attention. At the neural level, MTL population responses encoded performance errors — supporting a role for hippocampus and adjacent cortex in error monitoring — and this error-related population activity was significantly reduced during mind wandering. The result links a subjective state to a degradation of a specific MTL computation rather than to global inattention.

⚠ Small N, inherent to human intracranial single-unit work, and recorded in presurgical epilepsy patients — generalisation to healthy MTL function is an assumption.
